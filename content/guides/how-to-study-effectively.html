How to Study Effectively: 9 Techniques Backed by Research

Study Techniques · 8 min read · Updated November 2026

Most students study by re-reading their notes and highlighting textbooks. It feels productive, but research shows these are some of the least effective ways to learn. The good news: a few simple changes can help you remember far more in the same amount of time.

Here are nine techniques that learning scientists consistently find work, and how to start using each one today.

1. Active recall: test yourself instead of re-reading

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory instead of putting it in again. Every time you successfully remember something, the memory becomes stronger.

  • Close your book and write down everything you remember about a topic.
  • Turn headings in your notes into questions and answer them without looking.
  • Use past exam papers as practice, not just as reading material.
Tip: If it feels difficult, that's a good sign. The effort of remembering is what makes learning stick.

2. Spaced repetition: review at increasing intervals

Cramming everything the night before might get you through tomorrow, but you'll forget most of it within days. Spacing your reviews over time leads to much longer-lasting memory.

A simple schedule: review new material after 1 day, then 3 days, then 1 week, then 2 weeks. Free flashcard apps like Anki do this scheduling for you automatically.

3. The Feynman technique: explain it simply

Named after physicist Richard Feynman, this method helps you find gaps in your understanding:

  1. Pick a concept and write its name at the top of a page.
  2. Explain it in simple words, as if teaching a 12-year-old.
  3. Wherever you get stuck or use complicated jargon, go back to your notes.
  4. Simplify your explanation again until it is clear.

4. Interleaving: mix different topics

Instead of doing 20 problems of the same type in a row, mix different types together. For example, in maths, alternate between algebra, geometry and statistics questions. It feels harder, but it trains your brain to recognise which method to use, which is exactly what exams test.

5. The Pomodoro technique: focus in short sprints

Study for 25 minutes with full focus, then take a 5-minute break. After four rounds, take a longer 15–30 minute break. Short, clear sessions make it easier to start and help you avoid burnout.

6. Remove distractions, especially your phone

Studies show that even having your phone visible on your desk can reduce concentration. Put it in another room or turn on "Do Not Disturb" during study sessions. Your focus is your most valuable study tool.

7. Use dual coding: combine words and visuals

Your brain processes words and images through different channels. When you combine them by drawing diagrams, timelines, mind maps or flowcharts, you create two paths to the same memory.

8. Sleep, exercise and eat well

Sleep is when your brain moves new information into long-term memory. Staying up all night to study often does more harm than good. Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep, and even a 20-minute walk can improve focus and mood.

9. Study with a plan

Decide in advance what you will study and when. A written weekly timetable reduces procrastination because you no longer waste energy deciding what to do next. See our 4-week exam preparation plan for a ready-made schedule.

Final thoughts

You don't need to use all nine techniques at once. Start with active recall and spaced repetition, the two with the strongest evidence, and add others as they become habits. Studying effectively isn't about studying longer; it's about studying smarter.
