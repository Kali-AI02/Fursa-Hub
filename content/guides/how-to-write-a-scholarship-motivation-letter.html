How to Write a Scholarship Motivation Letter (with Example)

Scholarships · 9 min read · Updated November 2026

Your motivation letter (also called a personal statement or statement of purpose) is often the part of a scholarship application that decides who wins. Hundreds of applicants have similar grades, so the selection committee reads your letter to answer three questions: Who are you? What do you want to achieve? Why should we invest in you?

This guide gives you a simple structure that works for most scholarships, a full example, and a checklist to use before you submit.

Before you write: do your research

  • Read the scholarship's mission. Some fund future leaders, some fund people who will return to develop their country, some fund research. Your letter must show you match their goal.
  • Read the exact question. "Describe your leadership experience" needs a different letter from "Why this course?". Answer what is asked.
  • Check the word limit. Most letters are 500–1,000 words. Going over the limit can disqualify you.
  • Know your course. Name specific modules, professors or research groups that interest you. It shows real motivation.

The 5-part structure

1. Opening: a real moment (about 80–120 words)

Start with a short, specific story that explains where your motivation comes from. Not "I have always been passionate about…" but a moment that changed how you think.

2. Your background and achievements (150–200 words)

Show what you have already done: studies, work, volunteering, projects. Use numbers and results. This is proof that you can succeed.

3. Why this course and this university (120–180 words)

Explain exactly what you will learn and why you can't get it at home. Mention specific courses, research or opportunities.

4. Your plan after graduation (120–180 words)

Describe clearly what you will do with the degree: the job, project, organisation or community you will serve. Be concrete about the next 2–5 years.

5. Why you deserve this scholarship (60–100 words)

Connect everything to the scholarship's mission and end with a confident, simple sentence. No begging.

Example motivation letter

This is an example for a fictional applicant applying for a master's in Public Health. Use it to understand the structure. Never copy it. Committees recognise copied letters, and yours must be your own story.

When the health centre in my home district ran out of malaria tests for three weeks in 2022, I watched nurses guess which children to treat. As a volunteer data clerk, I discovered the stock-out had been visible in our records for two months — no one had analysed them. That moment showed me that in public health, data that is not used costs lives.

I graduated with a Bachelor's in Statistics (upper second class) and have since worked for two years as a monitoring and evaluation assistant with a community health NGO. I built a simple Excel dashboard that tracks medicine stock across 14 health posts, which reduced stock-outs by 40% in one year. I also trained 25 community health workers to report data by phone. These experiences taught me that good systems are simple systems — but I lack the advanced epidemiology and health-systems training needed to design them at national scale.

The MSc in Public Health at [University] offers exactly this. The modules in Health Information Systems and Epidemiological Methods would give me the tools I am missing, and Professor [Name]'s research on supply chains in low-income settings is directly connected to my work. Studying alongside professionals from many countries would also show me how other health systems solve the same problems.

After graduating, I will return to my organisation to lead the expansion of our stock-tracking system to all 120 health posts in our province, and I aim to work with the Ministry of Health to make district-level data dashboards standard within five years. In the longer term, I want to train a new generation of health data officers.

This scholarship invests in people who will return and strengthen their countries. My track record shows I turn ideas into working systems; this degree will allow me to do so for millions of people rather than thousands. I am ready to make that investment count.

Why this example works

  • It opens with a specific story, not a cliché.
  • It proves ability with numbers (14 health posts, 40%, 25 workers).
  • It explains the gap: what the applicant can't do yet and why the course fills it.
  • The future plan is concrete and realistic.
  • It connects directly to the scholarship's mission.

Mistakes that get letters rejected

  • Generic openings: "Since I was a child I have been passionate about…"
  • Repeating your CV without explaining what you learned.
  • Talking only about money: "I need this scholarship because my family is poor." Financial need can be mentioned, but it should not be the main argument (unless the scholarship is need-based and asks for it).
  • No clear plan after graduation.
  • Copying letters from the internet, or sending the same letter to every scholarship without changing it.
  • Spelling and grammar mistakes. They suggest you don't take the application seriously.
  • Going over the word limit.

Final checklist before you submit

✅ Answers the exact question asked · ✅ Within the word limit · ✅ Opens with a specific story · ✅ Includes achievements with numbers · ✅ Names specific course details · ✅ Clear plan after graduation · ✅ Linked to the scholarship's mission · ✅ Proofread by at least one other person
