How to Write a CV That Gets Interviews

Applications · 7 min read · Updated November 2026

Recruiters often spend less than a minute on a first read of each CV. Your job is to make it easy for them to see, quickly, that you match what they're looking for. Here is a simple structure that works for jobs, internships and many scholarship applications.

1. Keep it short and clean

  • One page if you are a student or have under 5 years of experience; two pages maximum otherwise.
  • Use one simple font, clear headings and plenty of white space.
  • Save and send it as a PDF named like Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf.
  • Skip the photo, date of birth and marital status unless the employer specifically asks for them.

2. Use this structure

  1. Contact details: name, phone, professional email, city and country, and a LinkedIn link if you have one.
  2. Profile (2–3 lines): who you are, your main skills, and what you're looking for.
  3. Experience: most recent first. Include internships, volunteering and part-time work.
  4. Education: degree, institution, years, and strong results or relevant courses.
  5. Skills: languages (with level), software, and technical skills.
  6. Extras (optional): certificates, awards, projects, leadership roles.

3. Write achievements, not duties

Most CVs list what the person was supposed to do. Strong CVs show what they achieved. Start each bullet with an action verb and add a number wherever you can.

WeakStrong
Responsible for social media.Grew the club's Instagram from 300 to 2,100 followers in 6 months.
Helped with data collection.Surveyed 450 households across 3 districts and cleaned the data in Excel.
Did customer service.Handled 40+ customer requests a day with a 95% satisfaction rating.

4. Tailor it to every application

Read the job or internship description and highlight the key skills and words it uses. Make sure those that truly apply to you appear in your profile and experience. Many organisations use software that scans CVs for these keywords before a person ever reads them.

5. No experience yet? Use what you have

  • University or school projects, with what you built or found
  • Volunteering, clubs, church or community activities
  • Online courses and certificates (many are free)
  • Personal projects: a blog, an app, a small business

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Spelling mistakes. Read it aloud, and ask a friend to check it.
  • An unprofessional email address
  • Long paragraphs instead of short bullet points
  • Sending exactly the same CV to every employer
  • Lying or exaggerating. It is usually discovered at the interview.

Final checklist

✅ One or two pages · ✅ PDF format · ✅ Achievements with numbers · ✅ Tailored to this opportunity · ✅ No spelling mistakes · ✅ Contact details correct
